# The Man Who Survived Two Atomic Bombs
**Format:** Faceless stickman explainer · **Length:** ~3:00 (≈460 words at ~150 wpm)

---

### [0:00–0:15] HOOK
**VISUAL:** Black screen. A single stickman appears. A bright flash fills the screen. Cut to a second flash.
**NARRATION:**
Imagine surviving an atomic bomb. Now imagine getting on a train, traveling to another city… and getting hit by a second one. Three days later. This actually happened. His name was Tsutomu Yamaguchi.

### [0:15–0:40] WHO HE WAS
**VISUAL:** Stickman in a hard hat with a clipboard. Map of Japan pops up. Dotted line from Nagasaki to Hiroshima.
**NARRATION:**
In 1945, Tsutomu was a 29-year-old engineer who designed oil tankers for Mitsubishi in his hometown of Nagasaki. That summer, his company sent him on a three-month business trip to Hiroshima. On August 6th, it was his last day there. Tomorrow, he'd finally go home to his wife and baby son.

### [0:40–1:10] HIROSHIMA
**VISUAL:** Stickman walking, whistling. Tiny plane in the sky. Something drops on a parachute. Huge white flash. Stickman flies backward, lands, covered in bandage-white patches.
**NARRATION:**
That morning, he was walking to the shipyard when he heard a plane overhead. He looked up and saw something drifting down on a parachute. Then — a flash brighter than the sun. He was about three kilometers from the blast. The shockwave threw him to the ground. His eardrums burst. The left side of his upper body was badly burned. When he looked up, a giant mushroom cloud was rising over the city.

### [1:10–1:35] THE JOURNEY HOME
**VISUAL:** Stickman in an air-raid shelter at night. Then on a crowded train, arm bandaged. Train arrives at a "NAGASAKI" sign.
**NARRATION:**
He spent the night in an air-raid shelter. The next morning, he made his way to the station, found one of the few trains still running, and rode it home to Nagasaki. Wrapped in bandages, exhausted, he had survived the unthinkable. Surely, the worst was over.

### [1:35–2:05] NAGASAKI
**VISUAL:** Office. Bandaged stickman talking with big hand gestures. Boss stickman with crossed arms and a "?" over his head. Window behind them. FLASH.
**NARRATION:**
On August 9th, he insisted on going back to work. He was telling his supervisor what he'd seen — one bomb destroying an entire city. His boss didn't believe him. "That's impossible," he said. "One bomb can't do that." And at that exact moment, the room filled with the same blinding white light. Once again, Tsutomu was about three kilometers from ground zero.

### [2:05–2:35] SURVIVAL
**VISUAL:** Stickman crawling out of rubble. Running home. Wife and baby stickmen safe. Then a calendar flipping years quickly. Old stickman with a cane.
**NARRATION:**
Somehow, he survived again. His bandages were torn off, and he fell sick with fever for over a week. But his wife and son had also survived. And Tsutomu went on to live a long life. He had more children, worked as a teacher and an engineer again, and in 2009 the Japanese government officially recognized him as a survivor of both bombings — the only person ever formally given that status.

In his later years, he traveled and spoke out against nuclear weapons, so no one would ever experience what he did — twice. Tsutomu Yamaguchi died in 2010 at the age of 93.
